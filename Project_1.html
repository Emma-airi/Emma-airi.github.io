LEO constellation coverage simulator

A validated Python model of a 528-satellite low Earth orbit broadband constellation — J2 orbit propagation, Walker layouts, and how many satellites a user on the ground can actually see.

Python NumPy / SciPy Orbital mechanics J2 perturbation Walker constellations Unit-tested
Independent study. The baseline geometry comes from the public July 2026 FCC filing for Eutelsat Next: 528 satellites, 24 planes, 1,220 km. This work uses public information only. It is not Eutelsat's design, data or analysis.

The problem

Low Earth orbit satellites move fast, so no single satellite stays over London for long. A broadband constellation only works if, at every moment, enough satellites sit high enough in the sky for a user terminal to connect to one.

This model checks exactly that. It builds the constellation, propagates every satellite including the Earth's oblateness, and counts what a ground terminal can see over a full day.

The interesting result

The filing does not give the inclination, so the model treats it as a variable and sweeps it. The mean number of satellites over London barely moves between 80° and 98° — it sits near 10 throughout, which would suggest inclination hardly matters.

The worst case tells a different story. The minimum number in view peaks at 6 for a near-polar orbit and falls to 3 at the edges of the sweep. A designer sizing a system for the worst moment rather than the average would care a great deal about that difference, and an average-only analysis would have missed it entirely.

Global picture

Near-polar constellations crowd together at high latitudes, because every orbital plane passes close to the poles. The effect is stark in a global snapshot: the same constellation that puts about 10 satellites over London puts more than 45 over the polar regions, where almost nobody lives.

Method

  1. Constellation layout. Walker star pattern, with planes spread over 180° of right ascension as used for near-polar systems. A Walker delta option is also provided.
  2. Propagation, two routes. A numerical route integrates two-body plus J2 acceleration with SciPy's DOP853 solver — accurate, but too slow for 528 satellites. An analytic secular J2 route advances the right ascension of the ascending node and the argument of latitude at their mean rates, which is fast enough for the whole constellation and is validated against the numerical route.
  3. Frames. ECI positions rotated to Earth-fixed coordinates, ground sites placed on the WGS-84 ellipsoid, elevation measured from the local geodetic vertical.
  4. Coverage. Count of satellites above the elevation mask, both for one site over time and for a global grid at an instant.

The last check is the one I would point an interviewer at. Each footprint covers a fixed fraction of the sphere, so the area-weighted global mean must equal n_sats × (1 − cos λ) / 2 regardless of how the satellites are arranged. It is a result the simulation cannot get right by accident.

Assumptions and limitations

Stating these plainly matters more than hiding them:

  • Inclination. 87.9° is an assumption close to the current OneWeb fleet, not a filed value — hence the sweep above.
  • Elevation mask. 25° is an assumed user-terminal limit.
  • Along-track drift. The secular model treats the initial elements as mean elements while the numerical model starts from osculating ones, so an along-track offset grows by roughly 80 km per orbit. Planes and radii still agree closely, so coverage statistics hold, but satellite timing would not. A Brouwer or Kozai conversion would fix it.
  • Earth rotation. A constant rotation rate, with no precession, nutation or polar motion.
  • Geometry only. No link budget, gateway visibility, inter-satellite links, outages or beam capacity.

Background

This grew out of my undergraduate rotating space station design project, where I wrote a two-body propagator for the station's orbit and listed J2 as future work. This project adds J2, scales the propagator to a full constellation, and adds the coverage analysis.

Baseline geometry sourced from Payload Space, “FCC Filings Show Eutelsat is Working on a New 528-sat Constellation Beyond OneWeb”, July 2026.
